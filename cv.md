## Current Role 
Yo Yehudi is Co-executive Director and co-founder of OLS (formerly Open Life Science), a not-for-profit organization dedicated to equitable global culture change in research, towards open community-driven principles. Yo successfully transitioned OLS from a 100% volunteer labor initiative to a grant-funded organization with over 800 community members across 6 continents and 60+ countries worldwide, running virtual online open science cohorts before and throughout the COVID-19 pandemic. OLS cohort graduates have gone on to receive Grants and Fellowships from The Lacuna Fund, EOSC-Life, Code for Science and Society, The Software Sustainability Institute, The Open Bioinformatics Foundation, The Awesome Foundation, and more.
Research and experience: Yo’s EngD (Doctor of Engineering) research focuses on human aspects of software development and open communities, connecting common threads that an open source research software engineer might encounter: examining mental models of biological data for biologists and bioinformaticians; common factors in open science community sustainability behavior; and qualitative interviews around barriers to sharing and re-using COVID-19-related data. 
Prior to this, Yo worked as Open Source Technology Lead (Data for Science and Health) at the philanthropic research funder the Wellcome Trust, as Research Software Engineer and Community Manager at an open source biological data warehouse at the Department of Genetics Cambridge, as senior leadership at an academic spinout company, and as a software developer and technologist in industry.
Scientific contributions
Case Study: Research case study in barriers to open science
COVID-19: An exploration of consecutive systemic barriers to pathogen-related data sharing during a pandemic. Transforming towards open scientific behaviors is facilitated by an understanding of real-world barriers people experience. Those who work with life-saving relevant data often face significant systemic barriers to accessing, sharing or re-using this data. In this study we interviewed data professionals working with COVID-19-relevant data including social media, mobility, viral genome, testing, infection, hospital admission, and deaths. These data types are variously used for pandemic spread modeling, healthcare system strain awareness, and devising therapeutic treatments for COVID-19. 
Barriers to data access, sharing and re-use include the cost of access to data (primarily certain healthcare sources and mobility data from mobile phone carriers), human throughput bottlenecks, unclear pathways to request access to data, unnecessarily strict access controls and data re-use policies, unclear data provenance, inability to link separate data sources that could collectively create a more complete picture, poor adherence to metadata standards, and a lack of computer-suitable data formats.
DOI: https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2205.12098 (Currently in press with Data and Policy)
Case Study: Open and Inclusive community transformation 
Building Inclusive Open Source Communities at the University of Cambridge and the Wellcome Trust
InterMine is an open source biological data warehouse based at the University of Cambridge, staffed by an interdisciplinary team of biological and computational scientists, and supported by a broad open source community of contributors. Over the five years Yo worked at InterMine, she grew the community contributor base from an ad-hoc set of contributors and collaborators, to a thriving group of heavily engaged interns, researchers, and coders. Successes were marked by the most recent five hires in the team being historic open source contributors to InterMine, as well as managing over twenty interns in four separate cohorts. 
InterMine’s participation in open source inclusion programs such as Outreachy were eventually followed by both the Wellcome Trust and the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative awarding funds to Outreachy to support open source computational researchers. The Wellcome Trust Outreachy sponsorship initiative was spearheaded by Yo Yehudi after she transitioned into research funding, and was later identified as a key case study of inclusive funding in the Wellcome Trust’s Anti-Racism program.
